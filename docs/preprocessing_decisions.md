# Data preparation and preprocessing decisions

Working document for the *Data* and *Preprocessing* sections of the report.
Every decision states **what** we do, **why**, the **evidence** from our data
and the **literature** that supports it.

All numbers are reproduced by `python main.py`:

- *Data* section: all 2000 English reviews → `results/data_statistics.md`
  (descriptive only, no decision is based on class differences in these tables).
- Evidence for the preprocessing decisions: **training set only** (1500 reviews) →
  `results/preprocessing_audit.md`, so that the test set does not influence any choice.
  Table names in that file start with the decision they support (D3, D4–D7, D8, D13).
- Document-term matrix of the training set → `results/bow_summary.md`.

Code: `src/data/cleaning.py`, `src/data/preprocessing.py`, `src/data/split.py`,
`src/features/vectorizer.py`.

## Data

**Source.** MAiDE-up (Ignat et al., 2025), file `all_data.csv` from the authors'
repository. Real reviews come from Booking.com; fake reviews were generated by GPT-4
with few-shot prompts that contained real reviews, the hotel name and the city.

**D1 — English only.** 2000 reviews: 1000 real (`source = 0`) and 1000 generated
(`source = 1`). The data are perfectly balanced: 500 per class × sentiment,
100 per class × city, 10 cities, 100 hotels, no duplicate texts.

**Sentiment.** Derived by the authors from the Booking.com score: 2–6 negative,
7–10 positive (no overlap in the data). It is kept for the positive vs. negative
analysis of the random forest. Scores are *not* used as features.
Score distributions differ between the classes within a sentiment (generated negative
reviews mostly score 2–3, real ones 3–4; generated positive reviews mostly 8,
real ones 9–10) — a possible confounder worth mentioning.

**Length.** Generated reviews are longer and more uniform: median 57 vs. 41.5 words
for negative and 48 vs. 24 words for positive reviews; the longest real review has
548 words, the longest generated one 172.

**Data quality.** The `na_up_review` / `na_down_review` flags of the raw file miss
5 empty fields, so emptiness is recomputed from the text. No JSON artifacts
(braces, key names, stray quotes) were found in the generated texts.

## Preprocessing decisions

### D2 — One document per review

A review consists of two optional parts: *upside* and *downside*. They are
concatenated into a single text. In a bag-of-words model word order is ignored anyway,
and one document per review keeps the representation identical for all models.

*Alternative not taken:* separate vocabularies for upside and downside
(*clean* in the upside ≠ *clean* in the downside). This doubles the feature space
for 1500 training documents.

### D3 — Placeholder parts are treated as empty

Parts that consist only of *nothing, none, nil, n/a, na, -, no, nothing at all*
(case-insensitive, trailing "." / "!" ignored) are replaced by an empty string.
Longer phrases such as "Nothing to complain about" are kept, because they are real text.

*Evidence (training set, number of reviews).* Placeholders appear in the part opposite
to the sentiment and almost only in real reviews:

| | upside placeholder | downside placeholder | upside empty | downside empty |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| real, negative | 5 | 0 | 41 | 6 |
| real, positive | 0 | 32 | 2 | 122 |
| generated, negative | 0 | 0 | 6 | 0 |
| generated, positive | 0 | 3 | 0 | 35 |

*Why.* This reflects how people fill in the Booking.com form, not how they write.
Without normalization the token *nothing* would become an easy "real" indicator,
concentrated in positive reviews, and could bias the positive vs. negative comparison.

*Limitation.* Truly empty parts cannot be normalized. They produce no tokens, but make
real reviews shorter, which count-based models can partly pick up.

### D4 — Hotel and city names are removed

The hotel name and the city of each review are removed (case-insensitive, whole-word
match, so e.g. *Rome* is not removed from *chrome*).

*Evidence (training set, share of reviews):*

| | real | generated |
|---|---:|---:|
| city name, before | 7.7% | 32.5% |
| full hotel name, before | 0.3% | 25.6% |
| city name, after | 0% | 0% |
| a word of the hotel name, after | 2.1% | 8.8% |
| the word *hotel*, after | 30.5% | 48.7% |

*Why.* The names were part of the generation prompt (Ignat et al., 2025). A model
using them would learn the data-collection procedure rather than properties of
machine-generated language, and would not generalize to other hotels.

*Limitation.* Only exact names are removed; shortened names remain
(e.g. "The Hotel 77 Seventy-Seven" for "Hotel 77 Seventy-Seven - Maison D'Art
Collection"), and generated reviews refer to "the hotel" much more often.

### D5 — Lowercasing

Standard for bag-of-words text classification (course lecture; Ott et al., 2011).
Without it the counts of the same word would be split over several spellings.
A stylistic signal is lost: 8.5% of real vs. 0.4% of generated reviews are written
entirely in lower case.

### D6 — Punctuation is removed

As in the course pipeline (`removePunctuation` in R `tm`). Curly apostrophes are first
mapped to the straight one, apostrophes are deleted (*didn't* → *didnt*), all other
punctuation and symbols are replaced by a space.

*Why curly apostrophes are normalized.* Real reviews use the curly apostrophe (’) in
13.6% vs. 2.4% of generated reviews — a keyboard/encoding difference, not a word choice.

*Limitation.* Punctuation carries style and is used in stylometric detection of
machine-generated text (Kumarage et al., 2023): exclamation marks occur in 14.1% of
real vs. 4.3% of generated reviews. Our word-based representation ignores this signal.

### D7 — Numbers are kept

*Deviation from the course pipeline*, which removes numbers.

*Evidence.* 22.8% of real vs. 4.0% of generated training reviews contain a digit.
Ignat et al. (2025) also report numerals among the most salient words of real reviews.
Numbers reflect concrete experience (e.g. prices, times), a genuine difference in
content rather than an artifact.

### D8 — Stop words are kept

*Deviation from the course pipeline*, which removes English stop words.

*Why.* The task is to distinguish *who* wrote a text, not *what* it is about.
Function words are central to stylometric authorship analysis and to detecting
machine-generated text (Kumarage et al., 2023). Ott et al. (2011) found more
first-person pronouns in deceptive hotel reviews and built their n-gram features
without a stop-word list. Jurafsky & Martin note that stop-word lists usually do not
improve text classification. Ignat et al. (2025) describe stop-word removal and
lemmatization only for their topic analysis.

*Evidence (training set).* Stop words are used very differently by the two classes
(frequency per 1000 tokens):

| | real | generated | generated / real |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| *we* | 10.8 | 1.6 | 0.15 |
| *i* | 15.7 | 5.1 | 0.32 |
| *very* | 13.8 | 8.1 | 0.59 |
| *the* | 74.0 | 102.6 | 1.39 |
| *were* | 8.5 | 14.5 | 1.71 |

Ranking all terms by mutual information with the class (as in the course), 3 of the
10, 11 of the 30 and 25 of the 100 most informative terms are stop words
(scikit-learn's English list), e.g. *and, the, were, was, a, of, with, we*.
Removing them would discard a large part of the signal.

### D9 — One-character tokens are kept

scikit-learn's default token pattern keeps only tokens of 2+ characters and silently
drops *i* and *a*. We use `\b\w+\b`, so that these stop words and single digits are
kept, consistent with D7 and D8.

### D10 — No stemming or lemmatization

As in the course pipeline and Ott et al. (2011) ("lowercased and unstemmed").
Word forms may carry style (tense, plural, *-ly* adverbs); reviews are short and the
vocabulary is small (4748 unigrams in the training set), so reducing it is not necessary.

### D11 — Term counts

Multinomial naive Bayes models word counts, so raw term frequencies are used for all
models, as in the course pipeline.

### D12 — N-grams: tuned by cross-validation

Candidates: unigrams, and unigrams + bigrams. Ott et al. (2011) obtained their best
results with unigrams + bigrams. In the training set there are 4748 unigrams vs.
35728 unigrams + bigrams (before sparse-term removal).

### D13 — Sparse terms: tuned by cross-validation

`min_df` ∈ {1, 2, 5, 10} documents (within the training folds); `min_df = 1` means
no sparse-term removal.

*Why not the 5% threshold of the course example.* It was chosen for IMDB movie
reviews, which are long. Our reviews are short: on average 51 tokens and only
38.7 distinct terms per review. With so few words per document, only common terms
appear in 5% of the reviews, so an aggressive threshold throws away most of the
vocabulary and a large part of each review (training set):

| `min_df` (documents) | 1 | 2 | 5 | 10 | 20 | 75 (5%) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| unigrams kept | 4748 | 2608 | 1319 | 829 | 495 | 134 |
| share of all tokens kept | 100% | 97.1% | 92.4% | 88.0% | 81.8% | 63.3% |
| distinct terms per review | 38.7 | 37.3 | 35.0 | 32.8 | 29.7 | 20.9 |
| uni+bigrams kept | 35728 | 10583 | 3593 | 1756 | 881 | 187 |

At 5%, more than a third of all text is discarded and an average review is reduced
to 21 terms. At the other extreme, 2140 of the 4748 unigrams (45%) occur in a single
review only; such terms cannot generalize and mainly add noise, which matters most
for naive Bayes. Mild thresholds (2–10 documents) remove most of these rare terms
while keeping 88–97% of the text, so the value is chosen by cross-validation from this
range, with `min_df = 1` as the no-removal baseline. Within a training fold of
1200 reviews the numbers are slightly smaller.

### D14 — No leakage from the vocabulary

D2–D10 transform each review on its own and learn nothing from the data, so they
are applied before the split. The vocabulary, sparse-term removal and feature
selection are learned from data: the vectorizer is the first step of every sklearn
Pipeline, fitted on the training folds during cross-validation and on the full
training set for the final model. The test set is transformed with the training
vocabulary only (as in the course: `DocumentTermMatrix(..., dictionary = ...)`).

### D15 — Train/test split

A single random split: 75% training (1500 reviews) and 25% test (500 reviews),
stratified on class × sentiment, so each of the four groups (real/generated ×
positive/negative) has 375 training and 125 test reviews. Stratifying on sentiment as
well keeps the positive vs. negative comparison on the test set balanced. The seed is
fixed (42) and the review ids of both sets are saved to `data/split/train_test_split.csv`,
so all models use exactly the same split. The test set is used only once, to evaluate the
five final models.

## Points for the Limitations section

- Remaining prompt artifacts: shortened hotel names and the word *hotel* (D4).
- Empty parts and review length differ between the classes and cannot be removed (D3).
- Lost stylistic signals: punctuation and capitalization (D5, D6).
- Review scores differ between the classes within a sentiment (Data).
- A single train/test split: the variability of the test estimates is not assessed.
- Few-shot generation with real examples by one LLM; results may not transfer to other
  LLMs or prompts.
